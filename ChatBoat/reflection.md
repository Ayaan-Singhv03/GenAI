# Project Reflection

I learned something visceral building this: garbage prompts produce garbage responses. When I first tried telling the AI to "be kind and supportive," it churned out the same generic tutor-speak I'd read a thousand times before. But the moment I got specific—layering in actual values, tone preferences, and real conversation examples—something clicked. The AI started sounding like *someone* instead of just following instructions. That's when I realized: intentionality isn't buried in the model. It lives in the prompt.

My approach wasn't magical—it was methodical. I built each persona step by step. First, I sketched out who they were: their background, what they actually cared about, how they naturally spoke. Then I added three solid examples—real-looking questions with answers that showed the tone I wanted. To keep things tight, I set hard rules: short responses, always end with a question, and no cheap motivational poster phrases. That last rule mattered most. I explicitly told the system: *don't improvise backstories, don't pad with clichés.* That constraint, counterintuitively, made the responses sharper and more genuine.

The hard part wasn't building the system—it was getting the data right. I kept hitting the same wall: how do you make a persona feel real when you're working from limited documentation? My instinct was to lock it down, stick only to verified facts, refuse to speculate. That bought me accuracy, but it also bought me flatness. A persona built on scraps feels like a persona. You can feel the edges. This is where GIGO really bites: no amount of clever prompt engineering fixes hollow source material. The constraint taught me something useful though—it's not about having everything; it's about being ruthlessly honest about what you don't know.

If I'd had more time, here's what I'd actually do: build a lightweight testing framework. Three simple axes—does this sound right? (tonal accuracy), is this true? (factual consistency), does this follow the rules? (structural compliance). Then I'd run the same batch of test questions repeatedly, watching for drift. I'd also tinker with temperature and token limits, because those dials matter more than people realize. There's a sweet spot where the persona's personality shines without becoming erratic. One more thing: I'd add explicit guardrails for the messy stuff—burnout, impossible deadlines, team conflict. Those topics need care. You can't just let the model improvise empathy.

The takeaway isn't complicated: prompt engineering is product work. You can't wing it. Building a persona that holds up requires intention, concrete examples, and real constraints. GIGO isn't a warning—it's the operating principle. The better your input, the better your output. Simple as that.
